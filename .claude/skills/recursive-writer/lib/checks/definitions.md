# Definitions

**Applies when:** always.
**Requires:** a term is defined only when the reader needs it, where they need it, in their words.
**Evidence:** the term, why this reader needs it, the reader's question at that spot ("what is this?"
or "how do I…?"), its first use, and its nearest look-alike.

- When: a term the reader hasn't met and can't infer (a product term, a word in an unusual sense).
  Don't define common or self-explanatory things. Another vendor's term at its first use in this
  article, even one that reads as plain English: a plain noun leads, the vendor's term follows.
- Where: at the term's first use. Open with a definition only where the reader arrives asking "what
  is this?" (a concept article, a section named after the term): relate it to something they know,
  then say straight away what it does for them. In a task section or a step, the purpose or action
  comes first. A value-first opening wins wherever it's quicker, as accurate, and easier to digest.
- Content: what sets it apart from its nearest look-alike; what the reader does with it, not its
  internal structure. Like with like: an entity is a kind of entity; a UI control enters as an action
  ("after you select…"); an entity isn't its contents.
- Wording: not circular; no taxonomies, tables, or glossaries for a concept.
- Exception to "no glossaries": when two look-alike terms are used together (Google account,
  advertising account), define both at the first use of either, side by side.
- Reuse or link an existing definition; don't standardize other articles' definitions in an edit.
  Never invent a new one, or present invented wording as established.
